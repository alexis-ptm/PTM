# Ad framework: core care to CTA

A full method for building a cold-traffic ad, from the value prop to testing. Use it before writing any script or ad body, and when diagnosing an ad that isn't converting.

## 1. Find the core care (do this before writing a word)

Strip the offer down to the one thing the prospect cares about. For an agency or lead-gen offer, get answers to:

1. How much is each customer worth to your client?
2. How long until the client cash-flows working with you?
3. How long until they're profitable?
4. Your top four clients: what changed for each of them?
5. All in (service fee plus ad spend), what does it cost to work with you?

Define exactly what the buyer is risking. Value is referential: they need a baseline (what they put in) to judge what they get back.

Example: they risk $5,000 all in. Their core care is turning that $5,000 into more than $5,000, as fast as possible. The ad has to land, as quickly as possible:

- what they'll get
- how fast they'll get it
- how little effort it takes
- how likely it is to actually happen

"It depends" is not an answer. Get the real numbers (for an agency: the close rate on booked appointments). Clarity converts.

### Justifiers

Then stack justifiers that deepen the core care:
- Customers re-up, so ROI compounds. Worst case, they get long-term customers.
- The ads build brand awareness that feeds their other services.

List every core care and every justifier before scripting.

### Cold-traffic offer checklist

- **Cognitive fluency:** understood in under 5 seconds. If they have to think, you've lost them.
- **Big problem:** keeps them up at night, costs money, blocks growth. Not a nice-to-have.
- **Easy to start:** low friction.
- **Clear timeframe:** three weeks or three years? Say it.
- **Outcome first, not mechanism.** Not "a 12-week program." The result they get.
- **But still a clear how:** enough of the mechanism that the outcome is believable, not the blueprint.
- **Believable:** the market thinks "yeah, that's possible."
- **Risk on the seller:** guarantee, performance pricing, pay-per-show. Cold traffic buys give-and-get deals, not brand trust.

## 2. Map core cares to objections

Every core care creates one **main objection** (the first worry) and several **secondary objections**. Counter each with proof.

Example, weight loss drug. Core care: lose weight. Main objection: safety (research, physical and mental effects, long-term harm). Counter: studies, FDA approval, medical supervision, upside vs downside. Secondary: needles (counter: these are thin needles; thick ones are what hurt), nausea (counter: side effects usually come from wrong dosing).

**Flip, don't neutralize.** Turn the negative into a reason to buy.
- Objection: "Your tutors are so young."
- Neutral (weak): "They're less experienced, but cheaper."
- Flipped (strong): "We get that a lot. They took the test recently so they know exactly what your student faces, and they speak your kid's language, so they connect and teach better."

## 3. Ad structure: Hook, Promise, Objections, CTA

Not a hard rule, but the default when unsure. The ad's job is to get the next step, not close the sale. Rough benchmarks: about 2% CTR on the ad, 7 to 15% conversion on the landing page.

**HOOK → AWARENESS / PROBLEM / RESULT → MECHANISM → OBJECTIONS → CTA**

1. **Hook.** Speak to one of:
   - **Core care:** "Consistent qualified leads without constantly posting content, spending an arm and a leg, or competing on price."
   - **Condition** (pain, goal, situation): "You can sell installs. That's not your problem. Your problem is you can only sell to the people you're actively getting in front of."
   - **Label:** "If you're running an HVAC business doing over $50k a month but struggling to get the lead flow you'd like, listen up."
2. **Promise / trust.** Back the hook with:
   - Case study: "We helped John do over $200,000 in new HVAC deals last month, strictly off the appointments we sent him."
   - Data: "Our average client closes 4 of every 10 live appointments we put on their calendar. For every $3,000 in, upwards of $30,000 back."
   - Mechanism: "Most companies rely on referrals to stay busy. Every company that scales past X has a pipeline they control that sends qualified appointments on demand."
3. **Objections.** One at a time, each with a short testimonial or explanation. "You're probably worried about consistency, because most agencies get leads one week and none the next. We take on the risk and front the spend."
4. **CTA.** Soft ask, framed so the next step feels easy. "Click below. There's a short form, and you can book time with my team to get this live in 48 hours or less."

### Example ad (the structure in action)

> By the time a homeowner Googles "HVAC install near me," four other companies have already called them.
>
> If you're relying on Google and referral traffic alone, you're getting the leftovers.
>
> What about the thousands of homeowners in your city whose system is outdated but haven't started searching yet?
>
> They're the real prize, and they're completely invisible to your competitors.
>
> Our marketing system identifies those exact homeowners. We target them with ads, reach out to them directly, qualify their intent, and book them onto your calendar as exclusive, uncontested appointments.
>
> We deliver 5 to 8 of these per day, we cover the ad spend, and you only pay a flat fee when you sit with them.
>
> If you want to own your market instead of bidding on scraps, click below. There's a quick video showing our system end to end, and a calendar to book a call about getting this live in 48 hours or less.

## 4. Consumption order and retention

People take in an ad in this order:
1. **Video or image** (most important, spend most effort here)
2. **Headline** (repeat the hook or the promise and outcome)
3. **Primary text** (who you help, what the offer includes)

- A big drop-off in the first seconds means the hook is weak.
- Retention should decline gradually. That's fine: the right people act early. Those who watch to the end are the furthest from sold.
- **Put the strongest objection handles, guarantees and promises near the end**, as a final nudge. Leading with a guarantee attracts guarantee-shoppers.
- Handle the objections your **ideal** buyer has, not the excuses of someone talking themselves out of it.
- Re-deliver trust and objection handling after the click (landing page, confirmation, pre-call content, sales process). In the ad people are scanning to decide if it's worth their time; they consume proof only after they've decided.

## 5. Ideation and testing

- Give the filmer example creatives of the format you want (whiteboard, a hook style). Visual people need to see it.
- Script detail depends on the talent: an overview works better for someone who knows the market and can riff; word-for-word for someone who can't.
- **Shoot a separate body for every hook.** Meta's Andromeda buckets near-identical ads together and won't push them evenly. Very different audiences need very different bodies; slight hook variations can share a similar flow.
- **New launch:** hit as many distinct angles as possible, from market research, sales calls, top clients and past accounts.
- **After a winner:** 75% of each new test is variations on proven winners, 25% brand-new angles.
- **Judge by closes, not cheap leads.** Real account:
  - Ad A: 30 bookings at $35, 5 closes at $6,000
  - Ad B: 12 bookings at $55, 5 closes at $6,000
  - Similar ROAS, but B closes about 40% of bookings and sales takes less than half the calls for the same deals. At scale that means more closes with fewer closers.
- **Milk a winning angle:** write text iterations of it, then change the environment, outfit, look, camera distance, camera quality, add a funny hook, studio shots, heavier editing, walking selfie.

## 6. Match the messenger to the message

Delivery can sink a great script or carry a rough one. The audience expects a certain messenger. Three things must match that expectation:
1. **Environment:** on a roof next to an AC unit, in a truck, an office, a penthouse.
2. **The person:** look, clothes, build. Do they look like who should be saying this?
3. **Delivery:** tone, confidence, slang, directness, profanity, casual vs corporate.

When all three match, it performs. When they don't, good copy dies. (Hormozi: intense, jacked, simplifies complex ideas, exactly who you'd expect.)

## 7. Filter with conditions

Almost every audience has good and bad prospects. Two ways to filter: **the conditions you speak to**, and **the conversion data you send back to the platform**.

- Speak to the day-to-day conditions of a handyman and you get handymen. Speak to conditions only the top tier lives with and that's who responds.
- Describe circumstances, not demographics or labels. Not "if you have a 750+ credit score" but "You've been set up well financially for a while. You've paid your bills on time, and the bank would probably approve you for whatever you need."
- Use their words. Do they say "job" or "estimate," "lead" or "appointment"?
